You keep the data, the code and the documentation — with a plain account of what the model does badly as well as well.

What we do

An autonomous recorder captures more sound in one season than a person could listen to in a year. We build the classifiers that find the species in it — including in regions existing models barely cover.

Millions of frames, most of them empty or set off by wind. We clear the empties, deduplicate bursts, tag what is left, and keep the timestamps and station data straight.

Transects, point counts, ringing records, plot surveys — often the longest series anyone holds, and often in one spreadsheet. We turn them into occupancy and abundance models you can defend.

Open Sentinel and Landsat archives already cover your site. We turn them, and your own drone flights, into dated maps of canopy loss, regrowth and change.

From sequencing reads to a species list: the pipeline, the reference database, and an honest line on what the reads can and cannot support. In development.

Questions

Anyone gathering data about ecosystems without a data scientist on hand — rangers, field biologists, community monitoring groups, small NGOs, conservancies and students. You don’t need to know what model you want, only what you collected and what you want to find out.

Audio from passive recorders, camera-trap images, transect and point counts, eDNA reads, GPS tracks, satellite or drone imagery, and spreadsheets kept by hand over many seasons. Messy and incomplete is fine — most ecological data is both.

You tell us what you have. We come back with what’s answerable and the smallest first step — and if a careful count or a good chart will do, we say so instead of building a model. We test on a sample, build, then hand over the outputs, the code and the documentation.

You own it, and we don’t reuse it without asking. Precise locations of anything poachable or collectable stay out of everything we publish. If you need a data-sharing agreement or an ethics review signed, send it — that’s normal.
